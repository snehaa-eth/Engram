# Keep the cascade, replace its engines

As of November 2026, the earlier AI-brain design is still the right shape, but about half of its algorithms are out of date. Hybrid retrieval, a cascade of increasingly expensive steps, a cached prompt prefix and a cheap router are all still standard practice. What changed is the components inside each slot, and one write-path idea is now obsolete. Four of the thirteen algorithms need a **replacement**: BERT-era cross-encoders now *lower* the accuracy of a strong modern first stage (MMTEB-R falls from 64.64 to 58.36), while Qwen3-Reranker-4B raises it to 72.74. Naive binary quantization is giving way to RaBitQ-class codes, which come with error bounds. Mem0 itself **abandoned LLM ADD/UPDATE/DELETE reconciliation in April 2026** in favor of an ADD-only write plus temporal retrieval. And hand-weighted Generative-Agents scoring has been displaced by validity intervals plus multi-signal fusion. Three more need an **upgrade**. The dense embedder should be an LLM-based model: Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B gains about 7.5 retrieval points over e5 at the same size. RRF should become tuned convex fusion. LLMLingua-2 moves off the query path and into ingest pre-compression, where LightMem reports 20–117x token savings. The other six survive as they were. Two results reshape the business case. First, LoCoMo and LongMemEval-S are saturated and gameable: changing only the judge prompt moved the same 1,540 answers from 55.8% to 81.4%. Second, Mem0's 2026 accuracy jump came with a roughly **4x increase in context tokens** per query, from ~1,764 to ~6,956. The competitive opening is therefore a published accuracy-versus-tokens curve under a fixed, audited judge, not another headline score.

Treat the vendor figures as claims to reproduce, not facts.

The pattern is consistent. Wherever a 2023-era *neural model* fills a slot, it has been overtaken by a 2025 model of the same size: the embedder, the cross-encoder, and to a lesser extent the sparse encoder. Wherever the earlier design used an *LLM to make a destructive decision on the hot path*, the field has moved that decision to asynchronous time: reconciliation and importance scoring. And wherever it used a *parameter-free algorithm*, the algorithm survives, and the improvement comes from tuning or from where it is placed: BM25, HNSW, RRF, MMR and PPR.

## The retrieval slots keep their shape but swap their models

**Embedders.** The upgrade is close to free. Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B is the same size as multilingual-e5-large-instruct, yet scores **64.64 vs 57.12 on MMTEB retrieval**. The 8B model reached **70.58 overall and No. 1 on the multilingual leaderboard** in June 2025, under an Apache license with open weights ([Qwen3 tech report](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05176v3); [Qwen blog](https://qwenlm.github.io/blog/qwen3-embedding)). Gemini Embedding scores 67.71 on retrieval and is the API alternative. The leaderboard has churned since. QZhou-Embedding and LGAI claim the top spot, and a 2026 guide credits gemini-embedding-001 instead, but none of these claims were verified ([QZhou](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.21632); [PremAI](https://www.premai.io/blog/best-embedding-models-for-rag-2026-ranked-by-mteb-score-cost-and-self-hosting/)). Build the embedder behind an interface and plan to re-embed every 6–12 months. A bigger gain than switching models comes from changing *what* you embed. Anthropic's contextual retrieval prepends 50–100 tokens of LLM-written context to each chunk before embedding and BM25 indexing. That cut the top-20 retrieval failure rate from **5.7% to 3.7% (−35%) with embeddings alone, to 2.9% (−49%) with contextual BM25 added, and to 1.9% (−67%) with reranking**, for a one-time cost of about **$1.02 per million document tokens** using prompt caching ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval)). Memory facts are exactly the kind of short, context-poor strings this technique targets. A fact like "prefers the earlier slot" is useless without knowing who prefers it and for what. Late chunking is a zero-LLM alternative for long documents: it embeds the whole document first and then pools each chunk, for a few percent relative nDCG ([arXiv 2409.04701](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04701)).

**BM25.** Keep it, but do not expect it to carry semantic queries. On the reasoning-heavy BRIGHT benchmark, BM25 scores **14.8 nDCG@10 against 36.9 for Reason-Embed-4B**. SPLADE-v3 barely improves on it there (15.6), even though it beats BM25 by about 6 points on BEIR ([arXiv 2509.02558](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02558v2); [SBERT](https://sbert.net/docs/sparse_encoder/pretrained_models.html)). BM25's job in a memory brain is exact-match recall: names, IDs, SKUs and rare words that embeddings blur. Mem0's 2026 pipeline still fuses a BM25-like keyword score for this reason ([Mem0](https://mem0.ai/blog/mem0-the-token-efficient-memory-algorithm)). Inference-free learned sparse models are an optional later upgrade. They only tokenize the query, so client-side latency stays at about **1.1x BM25** while they score about 3 BEIR points above SPLADE-v3-Doc (author-reported) ([arXiv 2411.04403](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04403)).

**Fusion.** RRF remains a reasonable default when you have no labels, but it is the weakest link. Bruch, Gai and Ingber showed that a convex combination of normalized scores beats RRF both in-domain and out-of-domain. A dense weight α of 0.6–0.8 helped consistently, and only a small labeled set is needed to tune it. RRF's k parameter, meanwhile, generalizes poorly ([arXiv 2210.11934](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.11934)). The BRIGHT reproduction adds a sharper warning. Fusing BM25 *helps* weak dense retrievers (BGE 13.8 → 17.3) but *hurts* strong ones (Reason-Embed 36.9 → 35.9), and normalized fusion hurts more, down to 31.1 ([arXiv 2509.02558](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02558v2)). Once you upgrade the embedder, the lexical leg should receive a smaller, tuned weight, and it should be gated rather than always fused at equal strength. Turning on entity-match boosting makes this especially important.

**Reranking.** This is the clearest replacement in the stack. In the Qwen3 evaluation, every reranker re-scores the top 100 from Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, whose own MMTEB-R score is 64.64. bge-reranker-v2-m3 dragged that down to **58.36**, and jina-multilingual-reranker-v2 to 63.73. Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B lifted it to **66.36**, the 4B model to **72.74** and the 8B model to 72.94 ([arXiv 2506.05176](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05176v3)). Two conclusions follow. Never put an old cross-encoder behind a modern embedder. And the 0.6B reranker buys only about 1.7 points over a strong first stage, so it should run *conditionally*, for example when the top fused scores are close. The 4B model's roughly 8 points justify an escalation tier, not a default one. The BRIGHT data agree: an LLM reranker over Reason-Embed moved it between **35.7 (worse, Qwen3-8B) and 40.3 (gpt-oss-120b)**, while the same rerankers lifted a weak SPLADE+BM25 first stage from 17.5 to as much as 33.4 ([arXiv 2509.02558](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02558v2)). Reasoning rerankers such as ReasonRank cost about **0.25–0.5 s per query on 4×A800** (secondary figure) ([arXiv 2508.07050](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07050v1)). That is unaffordable on every call, but fine for an explicit "deep recall" request.

**MMR.** It stays. Several 2025–2026 successors exist. AdaGReS does redundancy-aware greedy selection under a token budget, DF-RAG picks a per-query λ, and another line uses facility-location submodular selection. None has verified benchmark wins ([AdaGReS](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.25052v1); [DF-RAG](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2601.17212)). AdaGReS's *framing* is still worth borrowing: select under a token budget, not a fixed k, because tokens are what the customer pays for.

## RaBitQ, not a new graph, is the index-layer upgrade

HNSW is still the in-memory default in pgvector, Qdrant, Milvus, Redis and Elasticsearch. The 2024–2026 progress happened in how vectors are *stored*, not in the graph. RaBitQ (SIGMOD 2024) encodes a D-dimensional vector as D bits after a random rotation. It gives an unbiased distance estimator with a provable error bound and beats PQ "by a clear margin" ([arXiv 2405.12497](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12497)). Extended RaBitQ generalizes this to B bits per dimension and is asymptotically optimal in the space-versus-error trade-off ([arXiv 2409.09913](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09913)). The practical gain over sign-bit binary quantization is that the error bound tells you *which* candidates to rerank, so you no longer guess an oversampling factor. pgvector's binary-quantization guidance suggests about **80x oversampling** ([Timescale guide](https://smithery.ai/skills/timescale/pgvector-semantic-search)), while Turbopuffer reports reranking **under 1%** of its narrowed candidate set with 1-bit RaBitQ (vendor) ([Turbopuffer ANN v3](https://turbopuffer.com/blog/ann-v3)). Production adoption is broad. Milvus 2.6 ships IVF_RABITQ, at **864 QPS vs 236 QPS for IVF_FLAT at 94.7% recall**, but only with an SQ8 refinement step. Raw 1-bit codes reach just 76.3% recall, and refinement adds about 15 ms ([Zilliz](https://zilliz.com.cn/blog/rabitq-1bit-quantization-for-ai-infra)). LanceDB's 5-bit IVF_RQ reports **96.2% recall@10** ([LanceDB](https://www.lancedb.com/blog/newsletter-july-2026)). Qdrant 1.15 added 1.5-bit, 2-bit and asymmetric quantization because binary quantization handles values near zero poorly ([Qdrant 1.15](https://qdrant.tech/blog/qdrant-1.15.x/)). One equal-byte comparison found IVF_RABITQ and IVF_PQ "genuinely competitive" ([OraCore](https://oracore.dev/en/news/turboquant-does-not-hurt-search-quality-equal-bytes-en)), so RaBitQ is the better default, not a universal winner. Int8 stays as the simple, safe tier for rescoring. Matryoshka truncation compresses a different axis, dimensions rather than bits, so it stacks with either, though no source benchmarks Matryoshka together with RaBitQ.

The multi-tenant findings matter more than raw speed. Filtering one global HNSW by `tenant_id` is the weakest pattern, because selective filters disconnect the graph. Qdrant's own numbers show the size of the effect. At about 4% filter selectivity, plain HNSW reached **53.3% accuracy at ef=64 and only 71.1% at ef=512**, while ACORN-1 reached **97.2% at 13.86 ms** ([Qdrant 1.16](https://qdrant.tech/blog/qdrant-1.16.x/index.md); [ACORN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04871v1)). The fix is to treat tenancy and ACLs as two different problems. Tenants should be *physically* partitioned. Qdrant 1.16's tiered multitenancy keeps small tenants on a shared fallback shard and promotes large ones to dedicated shards while reads and writes continue. Turbopuffer gives each namespace its own object-storage prefix, so no tenant filter is needed at all ([Qdrant 1.16](https://qdrant.tech/blog/qdrant-1.16.x/index.md); [Turbopuffer](https://turbopuffer.com/docs/architecture)). ACLs inside a tenant change constantly, so they belong in payload or bitmap filters applied with ACORN or pgvector 0.8's iterative scans, never baked into the index structure ([pgvector changelog](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pgvector/pgvector/master/CHANGELOG.md)). Object storage carries one trap for a product with millions of small personal tenants: Turbopuffer reports a **cold p50 of 874 ms against 14 ms warm** at 1M documents ([Turbopuffer](https://turbopuffer.com/docs/architecture)). A personal agent that wakes after a week of idle time hits the cold path. Small tenants are better served by exact search over a compact local copy.

Memory is write-heavy, and graph indexes handle deletes badly. IP-DiskANN (2025) is the first graph index to process inserts and deletes in place without batch consolidation, at lower update cost than HNSW ([arXiv 2502.13826](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2502.13826)). pgvector shipped a fix for "possible index corruption with HNSW vacuuming" as recently as 0.8.3 in June 2026 ([pgvector changelog](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pgvector/pgvector/master/CHANGELOG.md)). The robust pattern is LSM-style. New facts land in a fresh segment that is searched exactly. Segments merge asynchronously into the quantized main index. Deletes become tombstones that are filtered at query time and removed during compaction. RaBitQ needs no trained codebook, only a rotation, which makes it friendlier to streaming data than PQ. That last point is an inference from how the method works, not a benchmark.

## Mem0 abandoned the reconcile step the earlier design copied

The earlier design's write path had an LLM compare each new fact against its 10 nearest neighbors and choose ADD, UPDATE, INVALIDATE or NOOP. That is now behind the frontier, and the strongest evidence comes from Mem0 itself. In April 2026 Mem0 replaced its two-call extract-then-reconcile pipeline with a **single-pass, ADD-only extraction**. Changed facts are stored alongside old ones "with temporal context", memory count "grows by design", agent-generated facts become first-class memories, and retrieval fuses semantic, BM25-like and entity-match scores in parallel. Mem0 also dropped its graph `relations` field in favor of a built-in entity collection. Its stated reason was that UPDATE and DELETE erased details that later questions needed ([Mem0](https://mem0.ai/blog/mem0-the-token-efficient-memory-algorithm); [migration guide](https://docs.mem0.ai/migration/oss-v2-to-v3)). The self-reported gains are large: **LoCoMo 71.4 → 92.5, LongMemEval 67.8 → 94.4, and temporal reasoning 51.1 → 97.0**. They come from the managed platform with "proprietary optimizations", with the judge model not stated, and at about **6,956 tokens per query** ([Mem0](https://mem0.ai/blog/mem0-the-token-efficient-memory-algorithm)). That token count is about 4x Mem0's 2025 figure of ~1,764 ([Mem0 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19413)), so part of the accuracy was bought with context.

LightMem (ICLR 2026) gives controlled, same-backbone evidence for the same direction. It soft-inserts memories at test time and reconciles them offline in a parallel "sleep-time" queue. On LongMemEval-S with GPT-4o-mini it scored **68.64, against 56.80 for full text, 61.00 for naive RAG and 53.61 for Mem0**, using up to **38x fewer total tokens and 30x fewer API calls**. Counting online cost only, it used 106–117x fewer tokens ([arXiv 2510.18866](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18866v4)). Its advantage is not universal: with Qwen3-30B on LoCoMo, full text beat it (74.87 vs 72.60). Zep/Graphiti's bi-temporal invalidation and Hindsight's separately versioned "evolving beliefs" network point the same way. The consensus is non-destructive writes, with "which fact is current" resolved at consolidation or at answer time ([Hindsight](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12818); [agentic-memory survey](https://github.com/lhl/agentic-memory)).

That does not contradict the earlier finding that update-on-write stores beat similarity search on changed facts ([MERIT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05441)). The failure MERIT measured was retrieval returning both the old and new value with nothing to say which is current. ADD-only plus *offline* invalidation fixes that without adding an LLM call to the write's critical path. The revised rule is to append at write time and invalidate during sleep. The tricky window is between the two, because a just-changed fact coexists with its predecessor. Rendering every retrieved fact with its timestamp closes that gap, which is exactly the "temporal context" Mem0 credits for its +29.6-point temporal gain.

RL-trained memory managers are real but not yet a product dependency. Memory-R1 trained an ADD/UPDATE/DELETE/NOOP manager on only **152 LoCoMo QA pairs** and reached **J 62.74 vs 45.68 for Mem0** on LLaMA-3.1-8B. But simply distilling GPT-5 trajectories into the same model with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) reached 58.76, so RL adds about 4 points over cheap distillation ([arXiv 2508.19828](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19828v5)). Mem-α's RL-trained 4B model, using typed core, episodic and semantic memory, scored **0.592 out-of-distribution vs 0.198 for MemAgent** ([arXiv 2509.25911](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25911v1)). The transferable lessons are cheap ones. First, *typed* memory beats flat memory. Second, Memory-R1's answer-side "memory distillation" step, where the model selects the relevant memories from ~60 retrieved before answering, adds **+4 F1** and works with any API model. Parametric memory (Titans, nested learning, sparse memory finetuning) remains research-grade and requires owning the weights ([arXiv 2510.15103](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15103v1)).

Generative-Agents scoring adds recency, an LLM-rated importance score from 1 to 10, and relevance with equal weights ([Generative Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03442)). It should no longer be the primary ranker. Its importance score costs an LLM call on every write. Its recency decay conflates "recent" with "currently true". And its fixed weights fail the same way untuned RRF does. The 2026 systems that lead on temporal questions use explicit timestamps, validity intervals and temporal query filters instead, together with entity matching. Keep a decay term only as a weak tiebreaker inside fused scores, and keep importance as a *consolidation trigger* (deciding when to run reflection) rather than a ranking term. This verdict is an inference. No 2026 paper isolates the scoring formula in an ablation.

Personalized PageRank keeps a niche but loses its default slot. No verified 2026 PPR successor turned up. Mem0 removed relation traversal from its default path, and the best LongMemEval-V2 system, which works over 25M–115M-token agent trajectories, is **AgentRunbook-C, a coding agent that greps and reads raw files, at 74.9%**, while frontier LLMs without evidence score at most 14.1% ([ICML 2026](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/67423)). The earlier cost data still applies. HippoRAG 2 indexes with about 8x fewer tokens than LightRAG and 12x fewer than GraphRAG, and it wins on multi-hop questions (2Wiki recall@5 of 90.4 vs 76.5 for dense retrieval) ([HippoRAG 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14802)). Keep PPR as an escalation for multi-hop entity questions, and add agentic raw-log search as a second escalation for agent-experience and procedural memory.

## Context management now saves more than token compression

Prompt caching is still the foundation. Anthropic bills cache reads at 0.1x base input (0.05x on Opus/Sonnet 5.5) and OpenAI at 0.1x ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching); [OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-caching)). Vertex AI applies a 90% discount to cached Gemini 2.5 tokens automatically, with a documented minimum of 2,048 tokens ([Vertex AI](https://cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/context-cache/context-cache-overview)). What changed in 2025–2026 is that providers now manage the *window* itself, and those savings exceed anything token-level compression offers. Anthropic's context editing cut tokens by **84%** in a 100-turn web-search evaluation. Its internal agentic-search score rose **29%** with context editing and **39%** with the memory tool added ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/context-management)). The Tool Search Tool reports an **85%** token reduction from deferring tool definitions ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/advanced-tool-use)). These are vendor-internal numbers. Independent work supports the direction, though. JetBrains found that simply masking old tool observations was **52% cheaper and 2.6 points better** than the raw agent on SWE-bench Verified, matching or beating LLM summarization ([arXiv 2508.21433](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21433)). ACON's natural-language compression guidelines cut peak tokens by **26–54%** with closed models ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/kang26b.html)). Smaller contexts often *raise* accuracy, consistent with Chroma's context-rot findings across 18 models ([Chroma](https://research.trychroma.com/context-rot)).

LLMLingua-2 therefore changes jobs rather than disappearing. On the query path it is risky: it fell to **0.513 on GSM8K at half length** against 0.910 for an abstractive gpt-4.1-mini compressor ([Cmprsr](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.12281)), and it destroys cache hits if it ever touches a stable prefix. On the *write* path it is excellent. LightMem uses LLMLingua-2 plus topic segmentation as stage one of ingestion, and that pre-compression drives much of its 20–117x token saving ([arXiv 2510.18866](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18866v4)). For a memory business that matters directly, because extraction tokens are the dominant cost of goods sold.

The TF-IDF + SVM router survives. LLMRouterBench evaluated more than 400K instances and found that several recent and commercial routers "fail to reliably outperform a simple baseline" ([arXiv 2601.07206](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07206)). The earlier evidence, **93.2% accuracy** on RAGRouter-Bench, still holds ([arXiv 2604.03455](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03455)). For closed models without logprobs, add gates based on verbalized confidence or self-consistency (Rowen, Self-Routing RAG). These save *retrieval calls* rather than tokens, because the probe itself costs a short generation, and verbalized confidence is "poorly calibrated in absolute terms" ([arXiv 2607.25600](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25600); [Rowen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.10612v3)). One conflict is unresolved. Provider compaction rewrites the prefix, so it invalidates the cache from that point on, and no source measures the net cost of the two together.

## The revised brain: append fast, consolidate asleep, escalate rarely

### Write path

The write path moves every LLM decision except extraction off the critical path. **Step 1** appends each message, document or tool result to an immutable, timestamped, encrypted episode log. Each item is tagged with a provenance class (user-stated, assistant-stated, inferred, or ingested from external content), and the trust-tier rules from the earlier design still apply: external content cannot overwrite user-stated facts. **Step 2** segments the episodes by topic and, for verbose content such as documents or tool logs, pre-compresses them with LLMLingua-2 at about 2x. User statements, numbers, IDs and names are never compressed ([LightMem](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18866v4)). **Step 3** makes a single extraction call per session chunk, as in Mem0 2026. It writes atomic facts from both speakers, and each fact is *self-contained and contextualized*: the extraction prompt itself adds the "who, about what, when" that contextual retrieval would otherwise add in a separate call ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval)). Facts are typed as world fact, experience, preference or belief, following Hindsight and Mem-α. Every write is ADD-only, with valid-from time, ingest time, speaker and source ID. **Step 4** indexes each fact into the tenant's fresh segment: a Qwen3-Embedding vector (stored at int8 or RaBitQ, with an fp16 copy for reranking), a BM25 entry over the contextualized text, and links to canonical entities. **Step 5** is sleep-time consolidation, run on idle or on a schedule. It marks superseded facts with an invalid-from time (Graphiti-style) without deleting them. It rebuilds entity profiles and versioned belief summaries with provenance links. It rebuilds the pinned profile block in a batch so the cached prefix rarely changes. It extracts OpenIE triples for the PPR graph only for tenants that use multi-hop. It merges fresh segments into the main index and propagates user-requested erasure through lineage pointers. Distilling a small, self-hosted extraction model from frontier traces is the cost lever to pull once volume justifies it ([Memory-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19828v5)).

### Query path

| Stage | 2026 design | Target brain-side latency | Uncached tokens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Keyed cache | Exact key on (tenant, ACL scope, normalized intent, memory version). Never fuzzy across tenants | 1–3 ms | 0 on hit |
| 1. Pinned profile | ≤1,000 tokens, rebuilt during sleep, placed in the client's cached prefix | 0 | ~0 (cache-priced) |
| 2. Gate and router | Rules + TF-IDF/SVM: none / lookup / multi-hop / raw-search; time-expression parsing into a validity filter | <1 ms | 0 |
| 3. Hybrid retrieval | Qwen3 dense + BM25 + entity match inside the tenant partition, filtered to currently valid facts (or an as-of time), convex fusion with tuned α | 5–30 ms | — |
| 4. Conditional rerank | Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B over the top 30–50, only when fused scores are close | tens of ms on GPU (estimate) | 0 |
| 5. Budgeted packing | MMR or a budget-aware selector; one line per fact with date, status, type and source; may return empty | 1–5 ms | lean ~800–1,500; rich ~3,000–7,000 |
| 6. Escalation | LLM query rewrite → Qwen3-Reranker-4B → PPR graph (multi-hop) or agentic grep over episodes (agent history) | 0.3–3 s (rare) | 2k–10k |

The client SDK adds one instruction that costs nothing: "select the relevant memories, then answer". This is Memory-R1's distillation step, worth about +4 F1 ([arXiv 2508.19828](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19828v5)). The packing stage now exposes **two token budgets** instead of the earlier single target. A lean budget around 1K tokens competes on cost, and a rich budget around Mem0's ~7K competes on accuracy ([Mem0](https://mem0.ai/blog/mem0-the-token-efficient-memory-algorithm)). The benchmark decides where the knee of the curve sits. All latency and token figures in the table are engineering targets, not measurements. For reference, Zep's measured retrieval is about 189 ms (secondary) ([Mem0 roundup](https://mem0.ai/blog/state-of-ai-agent-memory-2026)), and Memory-R1's memory search runs under 0.35 s at p50 ([arXiv 2508.19828](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19828v5)).

### Multi-tenant filtering and index tiers

Tenant isolation is physical, and ACLs are logical. Tenants with **fewer than ~50k vectors** get no ANN index at all. They get an exact scan over int8 or RaBitQ codes with an fp16 rerank, which gives 100% recall, makes updates trivial, and avoids the object-storage cold start. This threshold is an inference; no source benchmarks the crossover. Tenants from **50k to ~5M vectors** get an in-RAM HNSW on a tenant-scoped shard, with int8 or 2-bit/RaBitQ quantization. Tenants from **5M to 100M** get dedicated shards with IVF+RaBitQ (Milvus IVF_RABITQ, LanceDB IVF_RQ) or a quantized graph with inline vectors. Qdrant's inline storage served 1M vectors in 430 MiB at **211 QPS and 86.9% accuracy, against 20 QPS and 53.3% without it** ([Qdrant 1.16](https://qdrant.tech/blog/qdrant-1.16.x/index.md)). Every request carries a shard key. ACL filters inside a tenant use ACORN when selectivity is low, which works best when about 2–20% of nodes pass the filter (third-party guidance) ([vicinity](https://docs.rs/crate/vicinity/0.8.1/source/src/hnsw/filtered.rs)), or pgvector iterative scans, and they fall back to exact search when the filtered set is small. A Postgres-first stack is viable up to about 10M vectors per tenant with halfvec, HNSW, iterative scans and per-tenant partitions. Beyond that, pgvector's lack of RaBitQ and its weak on-disk support favor a dedicated engine. That boundary is an inference from pgvector's feature set. No shared semantic cache, shared index namespace or shared "global learnings" store ever crosses tenants. This is the cross-user poisoning path MINJA demonstrated ([MINJA](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03704v2)).

### Token-saving layer

The service should sell the token layer as part of the product rather than leave it to each client. The SDK enforces a cache-stable layout: tools, then system prompt, then the pinned profile, then volatile history, then the memory packet last. It enables provider tool-result clearing or client-side observation masking ([arXiv 2508.21433](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21433)). It exposes the brain through a small MCP surface (`recall`, `remember`, `forget`, `profile`, `search_raw`) so that tool definitions stay small. And it pairs provider compaction with the brain as the durable external store, because Anthropic's best result came from combining context editing *with* a memory tool ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/context-management)), and OpenAI's compacted state is encrypted and cannot be inspected ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/compaction.md)). LLMLingua-2 runs only on ingest and on large one-off retrieved prose after the cache breakpoint. TOON and other compact formats are worth testing only for large uniform tables, because one independent evaluation ranked TOON 9th of 12 formats for accuracy ([DEV](https://dev.to/ikaganacar/toon-benchmarks-a-critical-analysis-of-different-results-5h66)).

## A twelve-step learning path a Python builder can finish

Every algorithm below can be learned and prototyped in Python. The Rust-heavy pieces, such as the internals of Qdrant, LanceDB and Turbopuffer, are things you consume through Python clients, not things you need to write. The order follows the architecture: build a working baseline first, then add the pieces that move the benchmark.

## Judges swing scores by 26 points, so the benchmark must audit its own grader

The 2026 leaderboard is not a measurement of memory quality. Headline LoCoMo and LongMemEval-S scores cluster at **90–95%**: Mem0 92.5/94.4, Zep 94.7 on LoCoMo with gpt-5.4 as both answerer *and* grader, Mastra 94.87 on LongMemEval, Hindsight 91.4/89.6 ([CoreDotToday via braindetox](https://braindetox.kr/en/posts/memory_benchmark_scores_reliability_2026.html); [Hindsight](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12818)). Strong full-context baselines sit close behind: gemini-3.1-flash-lite scores **80.7 on LoCoMo and 81.6 on LongMemEval** with no memory system at all ([braindetox](https://braindetox.kr/en/posts/memory_benchmark_scores_reliability_2026.html)). The grader explains much of the spread. Under one fixed prompt, a gpt-4o-mini judge scored the same system **74.0%, Gemini 70.3% and Haiku 62.1%**. Changing only the grading prompt moved the same 1,540 answers from **55.8% to 81.4%**. Under Mem0's 2026 grading, vague wrong answers passed **45.4%** of the time, and Penfield found a judge accepting up to **62.81% of intentionally wrong answers** ([braindetox](https://braindetox.kr/en/posts/memory_benchmark_scores_reliability_2026.html); [Penfield](https://dev.to/penfieldlabs/we-audited-locomo-64-of-the-answer-key-is-wrong-and-the-judge-accepts-up-to-63-of-intentionally-33lg)). The datasets have their own flaws. LoCoMo has **6.4–7.0% wrong or ambiguous answer keys**, which caps achievable accuracy near 93.6%. LongMemEval's file was replaced in September 2025, changing 454 of 500 haystacks, so scores from before and after the swap are not comparable. And with 500 questions, the minimum detectable difference is about **4.5 points** ([braindetox](https://braindetox.kr/en/posts/memory_benchmark_scores_reliability_2026.html)). Some headline numbers are not even QA accuracy: Supermemory's "97%" is recall@20.

The revised plan keeps the earlier design's core: one public harness, every baseline configured the way its own vendor recommends, a vendor pre-registration window, multiple seeds, and full-context plus plain hybrid-RAG baselines run with the *same* answer model. It adds seven controls aimed at judge reliability. **First**, pin the judge model version and prompt, publish both, and grade every run with two judges from different providers, reporting their agreement. **Second**, measure the judge's own false-accept rate by mixing in planted wrong-but-plausible answers and vague non-answers. Any judge accepting more than about 5% of them is rejected. **Third**, have humans audit a stratified sample of at least 200 judgments per dataset, and report exact-match or F1 wherever answers are short. **Fourth**, separate retrieval from answering by scoring evidence recall@k against gold evidence turns, so that retrieval gains cannot hide behind a lenient judge. **Fifth**, never let the answer model grade itself, the setup behind Zep's 94.7. **Sixth**, report confidence intervals and refuse to claim wins smaller than the minimum detectable difference. **Seventh**, plot accuracy against billed full-price-equivalent tokens and against p95 latency, so that the ~7K-token and ~1K-token operating points are compared honestly.

The dataset suite moves to the benchmarks that still discriminate between systems. **LongMemEval-S** (post-September-2025 file only) remains the primary QA test. **BEAM at 1M and 10M tokens** exposes the temporal collapse that saturated benchmarks hide: even Mem0's 2026 system falls from **61.8 to 16.3 on temporal reasoning** at 10M, and contradiction resolution is only 35.7 at 1M ([Mem0](https://mem0.ai/blog/mem0-the-token-efficient-memory-algorithm); [BEAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.27246)). **LongMemEval-V2** tests agent-trajectory memory over 25M–115M tokens ([ICML 2026](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/67423)), and **MemoryAgentBench** tests learned memory construction ([Mem-α](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25911v1)). MERIT's updated-fact and act-on-retrieved-value metrics, PrefEval, a MINJA-style poisoning suite at realistic memory sizes, a cross-tenant leakage test that must score zero, and LoCoMo categories 1–4 (kept only for backward comparison, with their flaws disclosed) carry over from the earlier plan ([MERIT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05441); [PrefEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09597v1); [MINJA critique](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05504v1)). Write-side cost, measured as tokens and dollars per 1,000 ingested turns, becomes a headline metric, because the ADD-only, compressed-ingest write path is where this design claims its largest advantage over Mem0 and Zep. Ablations should switch off one 2026 upgrade at a time (embedder, contextualization, reranker swap, fusion tuning, sleep-time invalidation, ingest compression) so that each verdict in this report can be confirmed or reversed on your own data.

## Conclusion

The two years since the earlier design moved the frontier in a direction that favors a lean challenger. The big wins came from cheap substitutions: a same-size embedder that is 7.5 points better, a reranker that adds points where the old one subtracted them, error-bounded bit codes, and dropping an LLM call from the write path. None of these requires a research team, and all of them can be prototyped in Python in weeks. The incumbents have also shown their hand. Mem0's 2026 leap traded about 4x more context for accuracy, ran under an unstated judge, and still collapses on temporal reasoning at 10M tokens. So the open question in this market is no longer "who scores highest on LoCoMo". It is "who has the best accuracy per billed token on long horizons, under a judge that has been audited".

That reframing points to two non-obvious moves. First, sell the write path, not just recall. Ingest-side compression and ADD-only extraction are where the 20–100x cost gaps live, and they are invisible on read-only leaderboards. Second, ship the judge audit as a product. Publishing false-accept rates, two-judge agreement and accuracy-versus-tokens curves turns the field's measurement crisis into a moat, because an incumbent can match your scores far more easily than it can retract its own.
